\begin{enumerate}[series=q]
\item Show that every vertex of a connected nontrivial graph is covered by some maximum matching.
\end{enumerate}

\solution
Let $M$ be any maximum matching. 
Suppose there exists a vertex $v$ which is unmatched in $M$.
All neighbors of $v$ must be matched, otherwise we could increase the size of $M$, which is a contradiction.
Select any neighbor $u$ of $v$, matched to $w$ in $M$.
Then $M\cup\{uv\}\setminus {uw}$ is a maximum matching which matches $v$.
Hence, we can construct a maximum matching which matches any vertex $v\in V$.

\begin{enumerate}[resume=q]
\item Let $M_1$ and $M_2$ be two maximal matchings in $G$ (not necessarily bipartite). Prove that $|M_1| \leq 2|M_2|$. Infer that any maximal matching is at least half the size of a \emph{maximum} matching in $G$.
\end{enumerate}

\solution
Suppose for contradiction that we have two maximal matchings $M_1, M_2$ such that $|M_1|> 2|M_2|$.
Let $V_2$ be the set of vertices matched by $M_2$, so $|V_2| = 2|M_2| < |M_1|$.
Since the edges of $M_1$ are pairwise disjoint, each vertex of $V_2$ is an endpoint of at most one edge of $M_1$, so at most $|V_2|$ edges of $M_1$ meet $V_2$.
Hence some edge $uv \in M_1$ has $u, v \notin V_2$.
But then $M_2 \cup \{uv\}$ is a matching, contradicting the maximality of $M_2$.
Therefore $|M_1| \leq 2|M_2|$.

A maximum matching $M^*$ is in particular maximal, so taking $M_1 = M^*$ gives $|M_2| \geq \frac{1}{2}|M^*|$ for every maximal matching $M_2$.


\begin{enumerate}[resume=q]
\item Prove the following pairing lemma: Given any even number of nodes of a connected undirected graph, there is a way to pair them up and construct paths between the pairs that are edge disjoint. Moreover such a pairing can be computed in polynomial time. \footnote{$T$-joins}
\end{enumerate}

\solution
Let the given even set of nodes be $W$ and define $k = |W|$.
Call a family $\calP$ of $k/2$ simple paths \emph{feasible} if its $k$ endpoints (counted with multiplicity) are exactly the nodes of $W$, and let $\Phi(\calP) = \sum_{P \in \calP} |E(P)|$.
Arbitrarily partition $W$ into pairs $\{u_i,v_i\}_{i\in[k/2]}$.
Since $G$ is connected, BFS finds a $u_i$--$v_i$ path for each $i$, giving a feasible family $\calP_0$ with $\Phi(\calP_0) \leq \frac{k}{2}(n-1)$.
For a path $P$ through nodes $a$ and $b$, write $P[a,b]$ for the subpath of $P$ between $a$ and $b$.

\paragraph{Swap.}
Let $A, B \in \calP$ be an $a_1$--$a_2$ path and a $b_1$--$b_2$ path that share an edge.
Let $x$ and $y$ be the first and last nodes of $A$, traversed from $a_1$, that lie on $B$.
Both ends of a shared edge lie on $A$ and on $B$, so $x \neq y$.
Relabel $b_1, b_2$ so that $x$ precedes $y$ on $B$ traversed from $b_1$, and set
\[
    A' = A[a_1,x] \cdot B[x,b_1], \qquad B' = A[y,a_2] \cdot B[y,b_2].
\]
By the choice of $x$ and $y$, $A[a_1,x]$ meets $B$ only at $x$ and $A[y,a_2]$ meets $B$ only at $y$.
Hence $A'$ is a simple $a_1$--$b_1$ path and $B'$ is a simple $a_2$--$b_2$ path, with $|E(A')| = |E(A[a_1,x])| + |E(B[b_1,x])|$ and $|E(B')| = |E(A[y,a_2])| + |E(B[y,b_2])|$.
Since $A = A[a_1,x] \cdot A[x,y] \cdot A[y,a_2]$ and $B = B[b_1,x] \cdot B[x,y] \cdot B[y,b_2]$,
\[
    |E(A')| + |E(B')| = |E(A)| + |E(B)| - |E(A[x,y])| - |E(B[x,y])| \leq |E(A)| + |E(B)| - 2,
\]
where the inequality uses $x \neq y$.
Replacing $A, B$ by $A', B'$ leaves the multiset of endpoints unchanged, so the resulting family $\calP'$ is feasible and $\Phi(\calP') \leq \Phi(\calP) - 2$.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
Starting from $\calP_0$, while two paths of $\calP$ share an edge, swap them.
Every iteration keeps $\calP$ feasible and decreases $\Phi$ by at least $2$, and $\Phi \geq 0$.
Hence there are at most $k(n-1)/4$ iterations.
Each iteration takes $O(m + kn)$ time: mark every edge with the paths using it to find a shared edge, then perform the swap.
When the loop ends, $\calP$ is feasible and its paths are pairwise edge-disjoint.
Pairing each $w \in W$ with the other endpoint of its path gives the required pairing in polynomial time.
\qed

\begin{enumerate}[resume=q]
\item Show that if $G$ is a forest with exactly $2k$ vertices of odd degree, then there are $k$ edge-disjoint paths $P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_k$ in $G$ such that $E(G) = E(P_1) \cup E(P_2) \cup \ldots \cup E(P_k)$.
\end{enumerate}

\solution
Consider each connected component $T$ of $G$ separately. 
$T$ must be a tree. 
Since the sum of the degrees of all vertices in a connected component is even, $T$ must contain an even number of vertices of odd degree.
Let $t$ be the number of odd vertices in $T$.
By Question 3, we obtain $t/2$ edge-disjoint paths $P_1,\dots, P_{t/2}$ in $T$, where the $t$ distinct endpoints of the paths are exactly the $t$ vertices of odd degrees.

Let $F = E(P_1) \cup E(P_2) \cup \ldots \cup E(P_{t/2})$
Clearly $E(T) \supseteq F$.
Suppose for contradiction that $H := E(T)\setminus F\neq \emptyset$. 
Note that the vertices of $V(F)$ of odd degree are exactly the $t$ endpoints of the $t/2$ paths, which are exactly the vertices of odd degree in $T$.
Hence, all vertices $H$ are of even degree. 
Thus, $H$ is Eulerian and contains at least one cycle, which contradicts that $T$ is a tree.
Thus, we have shown that $E(T) = F$.


\begin{enumerate}[resume=q]
\item Given two nodes $r$ and $s$ in an undirected graph with \emph{nonnegative} edge weights, show how to use an algorithm to find minimum-weight perfect matchings to find a minimum-weight simple path connecting $r$ and $s$ with an odd number of edges. (Hint: Consider a new graph by making two copies of the original graph and connecting corresponding copies of nodes with zero weight edges; thus, an original node $v$ becomes two nodes $v^1$ and $v^2$ and the total number of edges is $2m+n$. In this graph delete the nodes $r^1$ and $s^1$ and find a perfect matching). Adapt your method to find a minimum-weight simple path with an even number of edges.
\end{enumerate}

\solution
Let the two copies of the original graph $G$ be $G^1$ and $G^2$, where the vertices $r^1$ and $s^1$ are already removed from $G^1$.
Refer to the combined copies without $r^1$ and $s^1$ as $G'$.

\begin{lemma}
    There exists an odd-length $(r,s)$-path in $G$ if and only if there exists a perfect matching in $G'$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    If there exists an odd-length $(r,s)$-path $P$ in $G$, then we can construct a perfect matching in $G'$ as follows:
    For each odd edge $uv\in P$, add the edge $u^2v^2$ to the matching. This matches all vertices in $G^2$ corresponding to $V(P)$.
    For each even edge $uv\in P$, add the edge $u^1v^1$ to the matching. This matches all vertices in $G^1$ corresponding to $V(P)$, except for $r^1$ and $s^1$, which are already removed.
    Finally, for each vertex $v\in V(G)\setminus V(P)$, add the edge $v^1v^2$ to the matching. This matches all remaining vertices in $G'$, giving a perfect matching.

    If there exists a perfect matching in $G'$, then we can construct an odd-length $(r,s)$-path in $G$ as follows:
    The vertex $r^2$ must be matched to some vertex $u^2$ in $G^2$, which corresponds to an edge $ru$ in $G$.
    Thus, the edge $u^1u^2$ must not be in the matching, so $u^1$ must be matched to some vertex $v^1$ in $G^1$, which corresponds to an edge $uv$ in $G$.
    Taking the corresponding edges in $G$ (e.g., $ru$, $uv$, etc.) gives a path $P$ starting from $r$.
    At any time, all non-terminal vertices of this path have both of their copies matched, so the path never extends to an intermediate vertex (i.e., never loops back on itself).
    Thus, $P$ must eventually reach $s$, since $s^2$is the only remaining vertex without a partner in the other graph.
    Odd edges of $P$ correspond to edges in $G^2$, while even edges correspond to edges in $G^1$.
    $P$ starts and ends in an edge in $G^2$, and thus is odd.
\end{proof}

Assign weights to the edges of $G'$ as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For each edge $uv\in E(G)$, assign weight $c_{uv}$ to the edges $u^1v^1$ and $u^2v^2$ in $G'$.
    \item For each vertex $v\in V(G)$, assign weight $0$ to the edge $v^1v^2$ in $G'$.
\end{itemize}
The weight of the minimium-weight odd-length path is equal to the weight of the perfect matching constructed in the proof of above lemma.
Any minimum-weight perfect matching in $G'$ produce some odd-length path $P$ as per the construction in the above lemma, and matching all other vertices to their corresponding partners only decreases the weight of the matching, due to the non-negativity of the edge weights.
Hence, the weight of a minimum-weight perfect matching in $G'$ is equal to the weight of a minimum-weight odd-length $(r,s)$-path in $G$.

Our algorithm will run min-weight perfect matching on $G'$, and return the corresponding min-weight odd $(r,s)$-path in $G$.

To modify the algorithm to find min-weight even paths, first add a new vertex $t$ to $G$, connected only to $s$. 
All even length $(r,s)$-paths in $G$ correspond to odd-length $(r,t)$-paths in the modified graph by extending by the edge $st$, so we can run the same algorithm on the modified graph to find a min-weight even $(r,s)$-path in $G$.
